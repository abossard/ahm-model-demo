# Visualizing slider survey results beyond a single line

Research date: 5 October 2026. The existing payload and rendering were confirmed from `src/survey/ui/src/model.ts` and `src/survey/ui/src/components/App.tsx`. This note recommends visualizations; it does not change the UI.

## Scope and what this answers

The survey collects one 0-100 integer value per question from a horizontal slider (midpoint 50 shown but unanswered until interaction), anonymously, with partial/reset answers and per-question answered counts that can be small and unequal across questions. The public results view currently renders this as 101 individual one-unit-wide bars (one per integer value 0-100), monochrome, each scaled to its own count, alongside a mean and a total count; at low per-value frequencies this produces the thin, barely visible spikes the request describes. This note evaluates primary-source-backed alternatives that use color and filled area to show disagreement, neutrality, agreement and polarization, and that stay honest when a question has very few responses. It does not propose code, a library choice, or a redesign; it hands evidence to the implementation session.

## Recommendation (ranked)

1. **Diverging stacked bar per question** for the main results list and for comparing questions against each other. Bucket the continuous 0-100 value into an odd number of ordered bands (for example 5), center the middle band on a shared zero axis, and extend disagreement bands left and agreement bands right. This is the published, purpose-built design for exactly "disagree vs neutral vs agree" comparison across many questions, and it is the direct answer to "colors, filled areas, not just a line." [Heiberger & Robbins 2014][hr2014], live reference implementation: [Vega-Lite diverging stacked bar example][vl-diverging].
2. **Filled histogram (bin-count skyline) as an optional per-question detail view**, when a question has enough responses to make several buckets meaningful. This is a plain histogram, rendered as filled bars or a filled step line over the bin counts, not a smoothed curve. It is the standard primary-source technique for showing distribution shape, including bimodality/polarization that a mean hides, and its bin width must be chosen deliberately because it changes the picture. [Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 7][wilke-hist]; mechanics: [Observable Plot bin transform][plot-bin], [D3 bin()][d3-bin].
3. **Unit/dot plot (one small mark stacked per response, at its exact value) as the small-n fallback**, used whenever a question's answered count is small enough that bucket counts or a density curve would overstate certainty. This is built directly from the existing per-integer count array (stack `count[v]` marks at position `v`); it needs no raw per-respondent records and no API change, since the count at each value is already known. [Wilkinson 1999, *Dot Plots*][wilkinson-dotplot]; closely related strip/jitter framing: [Wilke, ch. 9][wilke-strip]; mechanics: [Observable Plot dodge transform][plot-dodge], [Observable Plot dot mark][plot-dot].
4. **Optional compact overview: a one-dimensional heatmap**, one row per question, cells colored by bucket count/share, for scanning many questions at a glance. Treat this as a secondary, denser view, not a replacement for 1-3, because color-only magnitude encoding needs a legend and redundant text cues, and sparse small-n rows will look noisy. Mechanics: [Observable Plot bin transform, one-dimensional heatmap example][plot-bin].
5. **Ridgeline plots and smoothed density ("skyline") curves are evaluated and not recommended here.** Kernel density estimation is explicitly documented as unreliable for small samples and can draw support outside the valid data range; the same book that documents ridgelines also documents that a ridgeline built from histograms instead of densities "often don't look very good" once many small groups are stacked. Both problems match this dataset: a bounded 0-100 scale and potentially very small per-question counts. [Wilke, ch. 7, density estimation pitfalls][wilke-kde]; [Wilke, ch. 9, ridgeline mechanics and its histogram-based failure mode][wilke-ridge].

None of this requires a new raw-data API or exposing individual records. Every candidate uses the existing mean, answered count, and exact count at each integer value. Display buckets are derived on the client.

## Why a single mean line under-represents this data

A point estimate plus a thin marker collapses a whole response shape into one number and conceals whether the underlying opinions are consistent, neutral, or split into two opposed camps. Wilke labels the structurally similar "one point plus error bars per group" chart as a problem case specifically because "by representing each distribution by only one point ... we are losing a lot of information about the data," and because symmetric error-style indicators are misleading whenever the underlying distribution is skewed or bimodal, which a mean cannot reveal on its own [Wilke, ch. 9][wilke-strip]. The diverging-stacked-bar literature exists for the same reason: Likert-style results are routinely summarized by a mean even though two questions with the same mean can have opposite shapes (everyone picking "neutral" versus half "strongly disagree" and half "strongly agree"), and the diverging bar was designed to make that difference visible at a glance [Heiberger & Robbins 2014][hr2014].

## Candidates evaluated

| Candidate | What it shows | Fit for this dataset | Primary source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Diverging stacked bar (bucketed) | Share of disagreement vs neutral vs agreement, signed around a center axis | Strong: built for exactly this comparison, works at any n, compares cleanly across questions in a list | [Heiberger & Robbins 2014][hr2014], [Vega-Lite example][vl-diverging] |
| Filled histogram / bin-count skyline | Shape of the 0-100 distribution, reveals bimodality/polarization | Good for medium/larger n; bin width must be chosen deliberately and disclosed | [Wilke ch. 7][wilke-hist], [Observable Plot bin transform][plot-bin], [D3 bin()][d3-bin] |
| Distribution strip / unit (dot) plot | Every exact per-value count as stacked marks, no inferred shape | Best for small n; most honest option when counts are in single or low double digits; buildable from the existing per-integer count array alone | [Wilkinson 1999][wilkinson-dotplot], [Wilke ch. 9][wilke-strip], [Observable Plot dodge transform][plot-dodge] |
| One-dimensional question heatmap | Compact row-per-question overview, color-coded bucket shares | Useful as a secondary "scan all questions" view; needs a legend and is noisy at very small n | [Observable Plot bin transform, heatmap variant][plot-bin] |
| Ridgeline / smoothed density skyline | Stacked density curves per question or per group | Weak fit here: KDE is unreliable at small n and can imply data outside 0-100; the same source documents histogram-based ridgelines looking "busy and confusing" | [Wilke ch. 7][wilke-kde], [Wilke ch. 9][wilke-ridge] |

Stacked **area** charts are a closer relative of the filled histogram and the diverging bar, but area marks are defined for a baseline/topline pair interpolated along an ordered axis, and the source documentation is explicit that "points in areas are connected in input order" and that unordered or unsorted input "may produce gibberish" [Observable Plot area mark][plot-area]. Questions in a results list are not inherently ordered along a continuous axis, so an area chart is a better fit for the shape of one question's own 0-100 distribution (as a filled skyline) than for stitching several unrelated questions together into one area.

## Visual anatomy

**Diverging stacked bar.** Bucket each question's answers into an odd number of ordered bands across 0-100 (for example five bands of 20 points each, labeled by position, not borrowed Likert wording, since the source data is a continuous slider and not a native Likert item). Split the center band in half across a shared zero axis; stack the bands below center to the left (more disagreement) and the bands above center to the right (more agreement), as percentages of that question's own answered count. The reference Vega-Lite specification implements this with a `signed_percentage` calculation that halves the neutral band and offsets the stack so it straddles zero, and colors the five bands with a diverging, neutral-centered palette (`#c30d24` strongly negative through `#cccccc` neutral to `#1770ab` strongly positive) [Vega-Lite diverging stacked bar example][vl-diverging]. Each question becomes one horizontal row, which scales to a card or a table row and lines up across questions because every bar shares the same 0-100 percent scale and the same zero axis.

**Filled histogram / bin-count skyline.** For an expanded single-question view, bin the 0-100 answers (for example into 10-point buckets) and draw filled bars or a filled step area, with no curve-fitting between bins, sized by count or share. This is the standard histogram construction: "we can visualize this table by drawing filled rectangles whose heights correspond to the counts" [Wilke ch. 7][wilke-hist]. Bin width is not a neutral choice: too narrow and the chart turns "overly peaky and visually busy"; too wide and real structure such as a dip or a second peak disappears, so the same source recommends trying multiple bin widths before settling on one [Wilke ch. 7][wilke-hist]. Observable Plot and D3 document binning with explicit edges and counts [Observable Plot bin transform][plot-bin], [D3 bin()][d3-bin].

**Distribution strip / unit plot.** At each exact integer value with a nonzero count, stack that many small marks (dots or short ticks) above the axis instead of one tall thin bar. This is the chart type Wilkinson formally documents as the dot plot, distinguished from a histogram precisely by displacing individual symbols "in a direction orthogonal to the scale in order to prevent dots from overlapping," which for repeated values produces "a stack of dots resembling a density" without fitting any curve through them [Wilkinson 1999][wilkinson-dotplot]. Wilke's closely related "strip chart" technique (raw marks, optionally jittered to separate overlapping points) is the same idea applied with lighter-weight tooling [Wilke ch. 9][wilke-strip]. Both are directly buildable from the count-per-value array this app already has, since the count at each value is exactly the number of marks to stack there; Observable Plot's dodge transform is the documented, named mechanism for this kind of non-overlapping stacking and is explicitly introduced by contrasting it with a conventional histogram built from the same underlying values [Observable Plot dodge transform][plot-dodge].

## Working from the exact per-integer count array (no API change)

The parent session confirmed the actual current payload: 101 exact counts, one per integer value 0-100, plus the question's total answered count and mean. The current rendering draws all 101 as separate one-unit-wide bars scaled only to their own count, which is why sparse, low-frequency answers show up as thin, barely visible spikes: most of the 101 positions have a small count (often 0 or 1), so most bars are both thin by width and short by height. This is a rendering choice, not a data limitation, and every recommendation above is implementable against this exact payload with no backend change:

- **Re-binning for the diverging bar or the filled histogram is pure client-side aggregation.** Summing contiguous entries of the 101-count array into, say, 10-point display bins is arithmetic on data already in hand; it needs no new endpoint and no change to what the server computes.
- **Boundaries must include both endpoints exactly once.** A ten-bin display can use 0-9, 10-19, through 80-89, then 90-100. The last bin includes 100 and contains eleven integer values; it is still the tenth bin. Another option is ten ten-value bins through 90-99 plus a separate eleventh bin for 100. Show the chosen ranges, retain every count, and do not interpret unequal integer cardinalities as equal per-value density.
- **Markers should stay exact even when the display is coarsened.** The question's mean (and a median, if added) should be computed from the full 101-value array and drawn at its precise 0-100 position, overlaid on top of the coarser display bins or bands rather than snapped to a bin's center or edge. Coarsening the visual bars does not require coarsening the summary statistics that are already computed from the exact data.
- **A unit/dot plot or a diverging bar both consume this array directly and involve no individual-level exposure beyond what is already public**, since both start from counts, not from per-respondent records; this matches the earlier constraint against inventing a new raw-data API.

## Honesty of smoothed curves

Exact per-value counts do not require smoothing. A kernel density estimate adds statistical assumptions, including bandwidth and boundary treatment; having exact counts does not make KDE inherently invalid. For this UI, small response counts and the bounded scale favor bars or a step-filled area over an estimated density by default. Any density estimate must be labelled as an estimate, not presented as recovered exact response counts [Wilke ch. 7][wilke-kde]. Decorative interpolation is different from KDE, but still changes the shape between observed points:

- **Bars and bin-aligned steps directly represent the chosen bucket heights.** A linear curve preserves supplied values at its vertices, but its intermediate heights are interpolated, not observed counts. Do not label an interpolated curve as the exact count at every intermediate value [D3 curve types][d3-curve].
- **`curveMonotoneX` is a documented middle ground**: it produces "a smooth curve with continuous first-order derivatives that passes through any given set of data points without spurious oscillations," and critically, "local extrema can occur only at grid points where they are given by the data, but not in between two adjacent grid points" [D3 curve types][d3-curve]. In plain terms, it can round off the straight-line corners without inventing a peak or dip the counts do not contain.
- **Spline choices need explicit justification.** Basis curves approximate their control points; cardinal and Catmull-Rom curves use different interpolation parameters. Their intermediate geometry is not additional response data. Preserving supplied points or avoiding extra extrema also does not establish that a filled curve preserves histogram area. Prefer bars or steps when area represents response frequency [D3 curve types][d3-curve].

Observable Plot's own area and line marks expose the same named curve options as a `curve` setting, so this distinction applies whether the fill is produced by hand-rolled SVG path construction or through a charting library [Observable Plot area mark][plot-area].

## Continuous slider vs categorical Likert: a labeling discipline, not just a chart choice

The diverging-stacked-bar literature was written for genuinely categorical Likert responses (discrete labels such as "Strongly disagree" through "Strongly agree") [Heiberger & Robbins 2014][hr2014]. This survey's native data is a continuous integer 0-100 value. Reusing the diverging-bar or histogram chart types is legitimate, but any bucket boundary applied to that continuous value is a derived, chosen summary, not the respondent's original category. Label buckets by their numeric range or by position (for example "0-20," "81-100," or "toward disagree") rather than reusing Likert option text such as "Strongly disagree," which would misrepresent a continuous answer as if the respondent had picked a discrete option. The binning mechanism itself (Vega-Lite's `bin` property/transform, Observable Plot's bin transform, D3's `bin()`) is explicitly a quantitative-to-discrete transform applied on top of continuous input, which is the correct technical frame for what this chart type needs to do to slider data [Vega-Lite binning docs][vl-bin], [Observable Plot bin transform][plot-bin], [D3 bin()][d3-bin].

## Small-n honesty, empty state, and edge cases

- **Kernel density estimation (smoothed curves) should not be used at small n.** The primary source is explicit: "density plots tend to be quite reliable and informative for large data sets but can be misleading for data sets of only a few points," and separately, "they have a tendency to produce the appearance of data where none exists, in particular in the tails," including generating curves that extend into impossible values (the book's own example shows a density curve implying negative ages) [Wilke ch. 7][wilke-kde]. For a 0-100 bounded slider, an unguarded density estimate could visually suggest responses below 0 or above 100, which cannot exist.
- **Empty state.** A question with zero answered responses has no distribution to show; a bucketed chart or any curve drawn in that state would visually assert data that does not exist. This note recommends surfacing an explicit "no responses yet" state instead of rendering an empty or zero-filled chart, but the exact empty-state treatment is a design choice, not something any cited source prescribes.
- **One or two responses.** Do not draw a density curve or imply a continuous shape; a bucketed bar built from one or two answers will technically render but says very little, so the distribution strip (raw marks, explicit count) is the more honest choice described above [Wilke ch. 9][wilke-strip].
- **All identical answers.** A histogram or diverging bar will correctly show a single full bucket; this is accurate and requires no special handling, but any minimum-bar-height rule added purely for visibility must not fabricate spread that was not reported.
- **Unequal, sometimes-small denominators across questions.** Always display each question's answered count next to its chart. When comparing two questions with very different n, do not normalize both to the same visual area or height without disclosing the difference: Wilke's own worked example shows that scaling two group distributions to the same height (equal area under each curve) erases the fact that one group had far fewer observations, and that showing distributions "as a proportion of the overall" total, with the denominator visible, is the technique that keeps the comparison honest for unequal group sizes [Wilke ch. 7, unequal-n normalization][wilke-hist]. The diverging stacked bar already encodes each question's bars as a percentage of that question's own count, which satisfies this as long as the denominator is also shown as text, not implied by bar size alone.
- **Mean vs. median vs. dispersion vs. polarization.** A mean alone cannot distinguish "everyone near 50" from "half near 0, half near 100," both of which can average to 50. Any of the three recommended charts (bucketed bar, histogram, strip) exposes this directly, because the shape is drawn rather than collapsed into one number; whether to additionally mark the median or split out an explicit "polarization" statistic (such as the combined share in the two most extreme buckets) is a useful but uncited design choice, since no primary source in this review prescribes a single standard polarization metric for slider data.

## Color and accessibility

- **Use a diverging palette with a meaningful, neutral-colored midpoint**, not a plain red-to-green gradient. The reference diverging stacked bar example uses a five-step diverging scale running from a red-leaning hue through a neutral grey to a blue-leaning hue (`#c30d24, #f3a583, #cccccc, #94c6da, #1770ab`) [Vega-Lite diverging stacked bar example][vl-diverging]. ColorBrewer's live tool (from the same design tradition as diverging schemes generally) lets a designer pick and preview sequential, diverging, and qualitative schemes interactively; this review could only confirm the tool is live and exports a chosen scheme, not the specifics of its internal algorithm, so treat it as a picker to try rather than a cited technical claim [ColorBrewer][colorbrewer].
- **Avoid a red/green diverging pair specifically.** Red-green color vision deficiency affects roughly 8% of Caucasian men, 5% of Asian men, and 4% of African men by the cited estimate, and the first concrete recommendation in the field's own guide is to avoid the red/green combination in favor of alternatives such as magenta and green, combined with a second visual channel (shape, position, pattern) rather than color alone [Okabe & Ito, Color Universal Design][okabe-ito].
- **Color cannot be the only channel that carries meaning.** WCAG 2.1 Success Criterion 1.4.1 (Use of Color) requires that information conveyed by color also be conveyed through some other visual means; it does allow a hue-plus-lightness difference to count as that additional channel if the two colors also reach a 3:1 contrast ratio against each other [W3C WCAG 2.1, Understanding SC 1.4.1][wcag-color]. In this chart family, bar position (left of center vs. right of center) and bucket labels already provide that non-color channel, in addition to hue.
- **"Just a thin line" is also a documented contrast failure mode, not only a stylistic complaint.** WCAG 2.1 Success Criterion 1.4.11 (Non-text Contrast) requires a 3:1 contrast ratio for "parts of graphics required to understand the content," and its own normative guidance warns that "particularly thin lines and shapes ... may be rendered by user agents with a much fainter color than the actual color defined in the underlying CSS," recommending that authors "avoid particularly thin lines and shapes" or push contrast further to compensate [W3C WCAG 2.1, Understanding SC 1.4.11][wcag-nontext]. Filled bars/areas are inherently easier to bring into compliance than a 1-2px line precisely because they have enough rendered area for contrast to register reliably.
- **Text contrast is a separate requirement from graphic contrast.** Question labels, counts, and axis text must meet WCAG Success Criterion 1.4.3 (Contrast Minimum): at least 4.5:1 for normal text, or 3:1 for large text (approximately 18 point, or 14 point bold) [W3C WCAG 2.1, Understanding SC 1.4.3][wcag-text]. This is a distinct numeric requirement from the 3:1 non-text/graphic requirement above; do not assume meeting one satisfies the other.
- **Keep the palette small.** Qualitative/categorical color scales are described as working best with three to five categories; once a design reaches roughly eight to ten distinct colors, matching color to meaning becomes burdensome even for people with typical color vision [Wilke ch. 19][wilke-colorlimit]. A 5-band diverging scheme is within this well-supported range.
- **Avoid large, overly saturated fills.** The same source cautions that large saturated color fields make a figure harder to inspect carefully and can interfere with reading adjacent labels or comparing against gridlines [Wilke ch. 19][wilke-colorlimit]; favor moderate saturation for filled bars/areas, especially at the extremes of the diverging scale.
- **Tooltips, if added, need their own accessibility handling.** Any hover/tap detail popup (for example, exact counts per bucket) must be reachable by keyboard focus, not only mouse hover, and WCAG Success Criterion 1.4.13 (Content on Hover or Focus) further requires that such popups be dismissible without moving the pointer, remain visible while the pointer moves onto the popup itself, and persist until the user dismisses them or the trigger stops being relevant [W3C WCAG 2.1, Understanding SC 1.4.13][wcag-hover].
- **No animation is proposed in this recommendation**, so reduced-motion handling (`prefers-reduced-motion`) is out of scope here; it would only become relevant if a later implementation adds animated transitions between states, which this note does not suggest.

## Implementation fit

All three primary recommendations (diverging stacked bar, filled histogram, unit/dot plot) are expressible as plain rectangles and circles positioned on a linear 0-100 scale, which is well within native, responsive SVG or CSS; none of them require fetching more than the per-value count array, mean, and denominator the app already sends. The bin/stack/dodge mechanics documented by Observable Plot (`Plot.binX`, area and dot marks, the dodge transform) and D3 (`d3.bin()`, curve types) describe exactly these shapes of transform (counts in, positioned shapes out) and are useful as a reference for the math even if no library is adopted [Observable Plot bin transform][plot-bin], [Observable Plot area mark][plot-area], [Observable Plot dot mark][plot-dot], [Observable Plot dodge transform][plot-dodge], [D3 bin()][d3-bin]. A full charting library (Observable Plot, Vega-Lite, or a heavier option such as Plotly.js) is worth reaching for only if the number of chart variants, per-question faceting, and interactive behavior (synchronized tooltips, legends, zoom) grows enough that hand-rolled scale and layout math becomes a maintenance burden in its own right; none of that complexity is implied by the three charts recommended above. This is a scoping judgment for the implementation session, not a claim with an external citation.

## What remains a design preference, not a sourced fact

- The exact number of buckets (5 vs. 7) and their boundaries for the 0-100 scale, including which convention resolves the 101-values-into-10-point-bins remainder.
- The exact small-n threshold at which the view switches from a bucketed bar/histogram to a unit/dot plot.
- Whether to show a median tick or an explicit "polarization" statistic alongside the diverging bar.
- The exact empty-state treatment ("no responses yet" wording/placement).
- Specific hex values beyond the general guidance to use a neutral-centered diverging scheme with limited saturation.
- Whether the one-dimensional heatmap overview is included at all, or only the per-question bar/histogram/strip views.
- Whether any curved fill is used at all versus straight bar tops; if one is used, `curveLinear` or `curveMonotoneX` versus the heavier smoothing options is a rendering choice to make deliberately, per the honesty discussion above, not a fact this review can settle for the implementer.
